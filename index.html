The HAVE framework

Four principles. Plain language. No jargon.

Built on research into how expert entrepreneurs actually think. Made practical for people building on their own.

H: start with what you already have

Who are you? What do you know? Who do you know? Your expertise, your network, your reputation, even your constraints are real starting points. Someone with 15 years in any field isn't starting from scratch. They're starting from something most programs don't know how to use.

A: know what you can afford to lose

Instead of asking "what's my expected return?" ask: what can I afford to get wrong? For solopreneurs with mortgages, families, and careers, this changes everything. You don't need to bet it all. You need small experiments where the downside is something you can live with.

V: build with people, not for them

The people in your life, your colleagues, former clients, collaborators, aren't just your audience. They help shape what you build. When someone puts in time, makes a referral, or gives you real feedback, they become part of what you're creating. That's why most solopreneurs don't need to start with strangers.

E: surprises are information, not setbacks

Every plan changes. The customer you expected isn't the one who showed up. Most business thinking treats that as a problem. This framework treats it as useful. Good entrepreneurs aren't better at predicting the future. They're better at reading what's actually happening and adjusting.

The HAVE framework is built on Effectuation, a body of research by Professor Saras Sarasvathy at the University of Virginia's Darden School. She studied 27 expert entrepreneurs and found that they don't start with goals and work backwards. They start with what they have: who they are, what they know, who they know, and let things develop from there.

"To the extent that we can control the future, we do not need to predict it." Saras Sarasvathy

The research has been taken up by Stanford, MIT, IMD, and INSEAD over the past two decades. This framework takes that same thinking and makes it usable for solopreneurs, without the academic overhead.

Source: Sarasvathy, S. D. (2001). Causation and Effectuation: Toward a Theoretical Shift from Economic Inevitability to Entrepreneurial Contingency. Academy of Management Review, 26(2), 243–263. Further reading: Effectuation: Elements of Entrepreneurial Expertise (Edward Elgar, 2008) and the Society for Effectual Action at effectuation.org.

Practicing effectual thinking changes how your brain handles uncertainty, risk, and opportunity. Not as a metaphor. The research suggests these are real, measurable shifts in how your brain works.

It quiets your stress response

Your brain's alarm system fires when you face uncertainty or potential loss. The affordable loss approach works by putting you in front of manageable risk repeatedly. Over time, your brain stops treating uncertainty as a threat. You get calmer under pressure, not because you talked yourself into it, but because your brain adjusted.

It builds flexible thinking

Every time you ask "what can I do with what I have?" instead of focusing on what you don't have, you're building a different kind of thinking. Do it enough and changing direction stops feeling like a crisis.

It changes what feels rewarding

Goal-driven thinkers get satisfaction from hitting set targets. This framework shifts that reward toward the process and what's emerging from it. When plans change, and they will, you're not left empty-handed. You're already engaged with what's next.

It builds practical instinct

What people call gut instinct is really fast pattern recognition built up from experience. Effectual practice builds those patterns faster by putting you in unclear situations repeatedly, with a framework for reading them instead of freezing.

It changes what your mind does when it wanders

Most people default to anxious future thinking: what if this goes wrong? Effectual thinking trains a different default. Instead of worst-case scenarios, your mind starts asking who could help and what this could become. That's not a small change.

It changes how you experience stress

There are two kinds of stress response: threat and challenge. Threat shuts your thinking down. Challenge focuses it. The neurochemistry is similar but the outcome is completely different. Effectual practice, by putting you in hard situations with a way to navigate them, gradually shifts which one fires.

Effectuation is mental training for an uncertain world. The brain that develops through this practice is less reactive, more curious, and better at reading situations that don't have clear answers. Not because you told yourself to think differently. Because you actually practiced it.

Everything else you might want to know before getting started.

The HAVE framework is an entrepreneurship methodology for solopreneurs built on four principles: Have (start with your real assets), Absorb (make survivable bets), Value Exchange (build with people not for them), and Exploit the Unexpected (treat surprises as signals). It is rooted in effectuation theory, developed by researcher Saras Sarasvathy based on how expert entrepreneurs actually think.
Lean Startup and Business Model Canvas both start with a goal or hypothesis and work toward it. The HAVE framework starts with who you are and what you have, and lets the goal develop from there. It's designed for people building without outside funding, partners, or a pre-defined market.
Solopreneurs, career-changers leaving employment, independent practitioners (consultants, coaches, freelancers), and anyone who wants to build something real without depending on a single employer or client. It's also useful for people who aren't building a business at all, but want a more resilient way to think through uncertainty and change.
AI is treated as a native Have asset that amplifies in proportion to your existing expertise. It lowers experimentation costs (Absorb), supports richer co-creation conversations (Value Exchange), and enables faster pivoting (Exploit the Unexpected).
