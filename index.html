The HAVE framework

Four principles. Plain language. No jargon.

Rooted in effectuation, which is how expert entrepreneurs actually think. Adapted for people building independently.

H: start with what you already have

Who are you? What do you know? Who do you know? Your expertise, your network, your reputation, even your constraints are real starting assets. Someone with 15 years in any field isn't starting from scratch. They're starting from wealth that most frameworks don't know how to count.

A: the art of the survivable bet

Instead of asking "what's my expected return?" ask: what can I afford to get wrong? For solopreneurs with mortgages, families, and careers, this changes everything. You don't need to risk everything. You need experiments where the downside is survivable.

V: build with people, not for them

The people in your life, your colleagues, former clients, collaborators, aren't just your audience. They're the ones who shape what you build. When someone puts in time, makes a referral, or gives you real feedback, they become part of what you're creating. That's why solopreneurs rarely need to start with strangers.

E: surprises are information, not setbacks

Every plan changes. The customer you expected isn't the one who showed up. Traditional business thinking treats that as a problem. This framework treats it as useful. Expert entrepreneurs aren't better at predicting the future. They're better at reading what's actually happening and moving accordingly.

The HAVE framework is built on Effectuation, a body of research by Professor Saras Sarasvathy at the University of Virginia's Darden School. She studied 27 expert entrepreneurs and found that they don't start with goals and work backwards. They start with what they have: who they are, what they know, who they know, and let things develop from there.

"To the extent that we can control the future, we do not need to predict it." — Saras Sarasvathy

The research has been taken up by Stanford, MIT, IMD, and INSEAD over the past two decades. This framework takes that same thinking and makes it usable for solopreneurs, without the academic overhead.

Source: Sarasvathy, S. D. (2001). Causation and Effectuation: Toward a Theoretical Shift from Economic Inevitability to Entrepreneurial Contingency. Academy of Management Review, 26(2), 243–263. Further reading: Effectuation: Elements of Entrepreneurial Expertise (Edward Elgar, 2008) and the Society for Effectual Action at effectuation.org.

Practicing effectual thinking changes how your brain handles uncertainty, risk, and opportunity. Not as a metaphor. The research suggests these are actual, measurable shifts in how your brain works.

It quiets your amygdala

Your amygdala is your brain's alarm system. It fires when you face uncertainty or potential loss. The affordable loss principle works by repeatedly putting you in front of manageable risk. Over time, your amygdala stops treating uncertainty as a threat. You get calmer under pressure, not because you forced yourself to be, but because your brain adapted.

It strengthens your prefrontal cortex

The prefrontal cortex handles flexible thinking. Every time you ask "what can I do with what I have?" instead of panicking about what you don't have, you're working that circuit. Do it enough and pivoting stops feeling like a crisis.

It rewires your reward system

Goal-driven thinkers get their dopamine from hitting predetermined targets. Effectuation shifts that reward toward the process, the collaboration, what's emerging. When plans change, and they will, you're not left with nothing. You're already finding it interesting.

It builds expert intuition

What people call gut instinct is really fast pattern matching built up from experience. Effectual practice builds those patterns faster by putting you in ambiguous situations repeatedly, with a framework for reading them rather than freezing.

It changes what your mind does when it wanders

Most people default to anxious future simulations: what if this goes wrong? Effectual thinking trains a different idle mode. Instead of running worst-case scenarios, your mind starts asking who could help and what this could become. That's not a small change.

It changes how you experience stress

There are two kinds of stress response: threat and challenge. Threat closes your thinking down. Challenge focuses it. The neurochemistry is similar but the cognitive outcome is completely different. Effectual practice, by putting you in difficult situations with a framework for navigating them, gradually shifts which one fires.

Effectuation is cognitive training for an uncertain world. The brain that develops through this practice is less reactive, more curious, and better at reading situations that don't have clear answers. Not because you told yourself to think differently. Because you actually practiced it.
